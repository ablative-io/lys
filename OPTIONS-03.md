# Domain 03 (access and permissions): options, item by item

Each item from the Top ten of `03-access-permissions.md`, in order. For each: two or three options, what Cherie (rung 1, first MacBook this weekend) would see, which mistake from the reviews it avoids, which of the page's purposes it serves (what purpose, what problem, what value), my recommendation, then a second go after looking at it.

The page's purposes, said once so the options can point at them:
- **Purpose:** a person can ask for what they need, and the person who can say yes can say yes, without learning a rights model.
- **Problem:** today the way to ask is hidden behind reads that have nothing to do with asking, the words change from tab to tab, and a give leaves the person unsure it happened.
- **Value:** "make the right thing easy, attractive and obvious first" (Tom, 18 Oct 11:02). The right thing here is asking and answering.

Inside the page, Requests also fails whole if `/people` is refused, and the Ask form fails whole if `/grants` is refused. The gate itself is in `shell/NavigationAccess.tsx`, which is domain 01's file.

## 2. Passing access from one person to another is unreachable

Give appears only on grants that pass on to agents (`GrantTable.tsx:107`, `model.ts:207`); the administrator's form can only make grants that pass on to agents (`IssueRoot.tsx:92, 116`). The server takes a person as recipient when the source grant names people (`delegation.rs:89-95`), with the person as their own responsible (`admission.rs:128`). The Delegate form already lists people; nobody can reach it for that.

| Option | What Cherie sees | Mistake it avoids | Purpose served |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Give shows on every standing grant she holds that may be passed on, to anyone. The give form offers only the kinds of recipient that grant allows (people, her agents, or both), shows someone not yet active as present but not choosable, with the reason. The administrator's form asks "May they pass it on" with two ticks, "to other people" and "to their agents" | On her project: a Give button. Pressing it: "To", her colleague's name among the people. A grant that only passes to agents shows only her agents, so she is never offered a name the server will refuse | "Offers the server will refuse" (03, Wrong refusals 11); "Person-to-person giving hidden" (Wrong refusals 3) | Purpose: the person who can say yes can give directly, without the two-person request dance (14 steps, 03 "Non-administrator gives another person") |
| B. A, and a Give button on a person's own file ("Give Sam access") | Same, plus a second door from the colleague's page | Same | Same, but the file is `features/file` (domain 02): an ask, not mine |
| C. Only change the administrator's form so it can make person-passable grants | Nothing for Cherie unless she is the administrator; the Give button still hides | None of the reviewed ones; it repeats "only the administrator can do it" (03 Summary 2) | Problem untouched for rung 2 |

**Recommendation: A**, with B's file button written as an ask to domain 02. Also, in the same change, `pendingGrant.ts:47`: the retained-give reader refuses any record whose responsible is not the signed-in person, and a give to a person names the person as responsible, so an uncertain give to a person would block the form for good (03 Top ten 6). Opening the person path without that one line would build the trap the review found.

**Second go.** I read the give form as rendered with a person chosen. The path now works end to end: Give on her project, her colleague's name in "To", a suspended colleague listed but greyed with the reason in a line beneath, and the body sent names the colleague as their own responsible, which is what the server checks. Two things I found on the second look and changed: an existing test expected Ada to be offered on a grant that passes on only to agents, which is exactly the "offers the server will refuse" fault, so the test now says the opposite and why; and Delegate's remaining three inline styles went into `grants.css` while I was in the file. What is still wrong, and I am leaving it to the items that own it rather than half-doing it here: the form speaks the model's language. Its heading reads "Give part of owner of project:identity to someone", the person path shows the relation chips raw ("owner", "only.edit"), and two cards she did not ask for say "G/00000000" and "You may pass it on: human, agent". Item 5 (three levels) and item 4 (one meaning of role) are where those words get fixed; doing them now would mean doing them twice. The file's own Give is domain 02's, written as an ask.

## 3. A refusal's "ask for it" link loses what was refused

`lys-pass` builds the link as plain `#/requests` (`crates/lys-pass/src/refusal.rs:72`) though the refusal it sits in already carries `needed: { app, kind, id, action }`. The Ask form then lists only what she already holds, so she ticks "What I need is not in the list" and types a raw "Type of thing" and "Its name" (`AskForm.tsx:200-205`). Rust is not mine: the link half is a server ask; the reading half is mine.

| Option | What Cherie sees | Mistake it avoids | Purpose served |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Requests reads `?kind=&id=&action=` and opens the Ask form with every field filled in | The same form as before, already filled: "Type of thing: cambium.channel", "Its name: general", an action ticked. She only writes why | "A refused person arrives at Requests with nothing filled in and must type an internal kind and id by hand" (03 Summary 3) | Purpose: asking is one sentence of hers |
| B. The same link, but the filled form says what she is asking for as a sentence ("Post in this channel, on Channel general"), with only "Why" and "Until" open and the filled fields folded under "Change what I am asking for" | One sentence naming the thing she was stopped from, a box for why, and Request | Same as A, and also the raw-identifier mistake (03 "Raw identifiers on screen"; Cherie "will never read … grant") | Purpose and value: the right thing (ask for exactly that) is the obvious thing; changing it is possible but not in her way |
| C. A separate one-click "ask for this" page | One button and a reason box | Same as B | Same as B, but a second ask form: the duplication mistake (four copies of retained-request code already) |

**Recommendation: B.** It is A with the words fixed, in the one form there is. **Server ask:** `refusal.rs:72` sets the fragment to `/requests?kind=…&id=…&action=…`, each part percent-encoded.

**Second go.** I read the sentence as rendered for both kinds of thing. It said "You are asking to write to this resource on Project Lys", naming the thing twice, and "…in cambium", the app's id in lower case. Now the thing takes the place of "this resource" ("You are asking to write to Project Lys") and the app's name is capitalised ("post on Channel general in Cambium"). I also found, before any test did, a trap a real browser would spring and jsdom never will: a required field left empty inside a closed fold stops the form with no visible word. The link can name an action no single relation carries, and then the relation select is exactly that field. So the fold closes only when everything it hides is filled, and one test is built for that rule alone (a drift injection that folds whenever there is a link fails that test and no other). Still wrong: an app's actions have no sentences in the model, so "post" is said as its bare name; the capitalised app id is not the app's display name ("Cambium" happens to match); and the unfolded fields still say "Type of thing" and "reader · read", which item 5 takes. Nothing here works for anyone until the server ask lands, because `lys-pass` still sends the bare `#/requests`; with it, a refused person writes one sentence of why and presses Request.

CONSENSUS has decided: "role" is used only for the grant's bundle, and the Roles page's object is a "job".

| Option | What Cherie sees | Mistake it avoids | Purpose served |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. Addresses (`#/roles`) and the service's names stay, so no link breaks | She never meets "role" unless she gives access in an app that names roles, and then it means one thing | "Role means two opposite things" (03 Top ten 4; glossary "Role", "Access template") | Problem: one word, one meaning; value: nothing to learn |

**Recommendation: A and B.** C is written as an ask to 01 for when it suits them. The review's other half, "let assigning a job issue its access templates", is a change to what the service does when a job is assigned, so it is a server ask, not a word change.

**Second go.** I read the Jobs pages as rendered, list, one job, its holders and both forms. A test now reads every word and label on the Jobs pages and fails on any "role" (it fails on the old code). What is still wrong: the address is `#/roles` and the rail and palette still say "Roles" (01's files, asked); the table still shows a template's access level raw ("viewer"), which item 5's three words should replace; and the real problem underneath the word is unchanged: a job still gives nothing, so a manager does two steps for one intent. ## 5. "They may" offers 84 options, each a paragraph long

The administrator's "They may" select lists every relation in the model, 4 named and one `only.<action>` for each of about 80 actions, each labelled with all its actions' sentences joined (`IssueRoot.tsx:111`, `action-words.ts:20-21`); the Ask form shows one checkbox per askable action (`AskForm.tsx:210`); the give-to-a-person path shows raw relation chips, "owner", "only.edit" (`Delegate.tsx:266-273`). CONSENSUS: "They may" offers view, edit, own; specific actions fold away under "more".

| Option | What Cherie sees | Mistake it avoids | Purpose served |
|---|---|---|---|
| A. One small picker in `features/grants`, used by all three forms: three choices, "View it", "Edit it", "Own it", each with a line of what it means said from the actions the model gives it ("See it", "See it and change it", "See it, change it and give others access to it"); under "More: one specific thing" a short select grouped by area, one plain sentence per action. An app's kind, which has its own few relations, lists those by name | Three choices she can read in a breath. The 80 actions exist, folded, one sentence each | 84-option select (03 Top ten 5); raw relation names on screen (03 "Raw identifiers"); words that could drift from the model, since each line is built from the model's own actions (CLAUDE.md, second party) | Purpose: deciding what someone may do is one choice of three |
| B. Only the administrator's form gets the three levels | The administrator's form reads well; asking and giving to a person still show the long lists and raw chips | 84 options, in one place | Half: the asker (rung 1) is the one who meets the Ask form most |
| C. Levels with no "more" at all | Three choices and nothing else | Everything above | Too far: an agent is never given a wider relation (`Delegate.tsx:261`), and a person asking for one exact act (item 3) must still be able to |

**Recommendation: A.** One picker, three places, so the words are the same wherever access is chosen. Agents keep the per-action picker they have (an agent is never given a level), so only the person paths change.

**Second go.** I read both forms as rendered. The administrator's "They may" is now three rows, "View it: See it.", "Edit it: See it and change it.", "Own it: See it, change it and give others access to it.", with the 80 single actions folded under More, grouped by area, one sentence each, instead of an 84-option select whose "editor" option was about 80 sentences long. Three things the second look changed. First, on the Ask form the fold said "one specific thing they may do" while she is asking for herself; its words now follow the form ("More: only some specific actions"). Second, an app kind with an `owner` and a `member` had its `owner` read as a level and its `member` filed under More; the levels now apply only where the model has all three, and an app's own relations are listed by name. Third, choosing a different kind of thing now clears the access chosen, since a relation belongs to its kind; five tests that chose the access before the kind were reordered. The words are built from each level's actions, and a drift injection that keys them on the name instead fails exactly the test built for it. I first left "Edit it: See it and change it" as it was, though the shipped `editor` also carries every agent and budget action; I would rather the line said so than a person find out, so a level now counts what else it carries ("See it, change it and 80 other actions."), with its own test. Still wrong: the More select is still about 80 options long in the real model, grouped but not searchable; and an app's relations are said by their bare names ("member: read, post") because the model gives app actions no sentences.
